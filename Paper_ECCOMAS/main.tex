\summary{A physics-aware machine learning surrogate is presented for the
prediction of aerodynamic surface coefficients $[C_p, C_{fx}, C_{fy},
C_{fz}]$ on a transonic transport configuration, requiring no CFD solver
at inference.  The model, AeroSurrogate, couples a spatial backbone
with three heads: an eight-layer message-passing graph neural network
over a $k$-nearest-neighbour graph of the surface ($k=8$) maps every
node to a 256-dimensional latent, from which a shock indicator estimates
the probability that the node lies inside the supersonic pocket, a
shock-gated Mixture of Experts predicts $C_p$, and an independent head
predicts the friction vector as a magnitude and a unit direction.  The
four smooth experts specialise spatially, their mean routing weights
differing between shock and non-shock nodes by a total variation of
$0.3581$, while a dedicated shock expert contributes an additive
residual scaled by the predicted shock probability, providing an
explicit mechanism for the pressure discontinuity that smooth networks
systematically blur; ablating that residual at inference triples the
$C_p$ mean absolute error on shock nodes, from $0.0458$ to $0.1370$.
All shock labels are derived from real RANS surface pressures via the
critical pressure coefficient.  No isentropic pseudo-labels are used.
Trained on RANS surface data of the ONERA CRM WBPN database spanning
Mach $0.30$--$0.96$ and angle of attack from $-15^{\circ}$ to
$+15^{\circ}$, and then fine-tuned head-only with the backbone frozen,
the model achieves $R^{2}=0.9729$ for $C_p$ and $R^{2}$ between $0.9027$
and $0.9209$ for the skin-friction components over 36\,508\,360 held-out
surface points from 140 simulations that took no part in model
selection, with $4.6\times 10^{6}$ parameters.  A closed-form shock
criterion recovered by symbolic regression from the same physics
labels is reported separately, as a
result about the point-wise predecessor of this model: substituting it
there for the neural gate costs $0.0085$ in $R^{2}(C_p)$ and $0.1005$ in
$R^{2}(C_{fy})$.  The same distillation applied to the spatial
indicator independently recovers that expression's core (the
critical pressure coefficient inside an exponential over span-wise
station) but attains only $R^{2}=0.4095$ against the indicator's
output where an unconstrained model on the same nine features reaches
$0.8850$, and substituting it costs $0.1096$ of shock-subset
$R^{2}(C_p)$ against $0.0168$ for the corresponding substitution in
the point-wise model: interpretability grows more expensive as the
indicator grows richer.}

\section{INTRODUCTION}
% ================================================================

Transonic aerodynamic flows are characterised by the coexistence of
subsonic and supersonic regions separated by shock waves.  In cruise
configurations, normal shocks on wing surfaces produce strong adverse
pressure gradients that drive boundary-layer thickening, flow
separation, and buffet onset.  These phenomena are central to the
determination of drag-divergence Mach number and the structural loads
envelope~\cite{Vassberg2008,Levy2013}.  Fast and accurate prediction
of surface aerodynamic loading across the full flight envelope is
therefore a fundamental requirement for aerodynamic analysis pipelines
that must operate at high frequency, such as adjoint-based shape
optimisation, digital-twin monitoring, or real-time loads estimation.

Reynolds-Averaged Navier--Stokes (RANS) solvers provide physical
ground truth for shock structure but at a computational cost of
$\mathcal{O}(10^{3}$--$10^{4})$ CPU-hours per operating condition.
Machine learning (ML) surrogate models have emerged as a practical
alternative for rapid aerodynamic
prediction~\cite{Han2018,Bhatnagar2019,Sekar2019}.  A central
challenge in transonic aerodynamics is regime non-uniformity: the
relationship between surface geometry, flight condition, and
aerodynamic loading changes qualitatively across subsonic, transonic,
and separated flow regimes.  A single multi-layer perceptron (MLP)
trained on data spanning all regimes learns a smooth interpolant that
systematically blurs shock discontinuities, underestimating peak
suction and misrepresenting the pressure-recovery gradient aft of the
shock (Figure~\ref{fig:pathology}).  This smoothing pathology is
structural: a smooth network can
only approximate a step change in $C_p$ with an error proportional to
its activation range, and gradient-based training actively favours
the smeared solution because it reduces average error over the
predominantly smooth surface.  In Figure~\ref{fig:pathology} the
points above the dotted line, where $C_p$ falls below the critical
value $C_p^{\mathrm{crit}}$, form the supersonic pocket; at the
transonic freestream Mach numbers sketched there they carry the shock
label of Eq.~(\ref{eq:labels}).

Existing ML shock indicators address discontinuity detection at the
volumetric field level.  Ray and Hesthaven~\cite{Ray2018,Ray2019}
pioneered neural network troubled-cell indicators for finite-element
solution fields; Discacciati et al.~\cite{Discacciati2020} and
Schwander et al.~\cite{Schwander2021} extended these with
convolutional architectures, and Morgan et al.~\cite{Morgan2020}
developed field-level indicators for Lagrangian hydrodynamics.  Two
limitations are shared across these approaches: (i)~they require
volumetric field data inaccessible in the surface-only measurement
context of experimental aerodynamics or flight test, and (ii)~they
commonly rely on pseudo-labels derived from isentropic assumptions
applied to input features, introducing a definitional circularity.
Physics-informed neural networks~\cite{Raissi2019,Karniadakis2021}
offer an alternative route but require embedding governing equations
explicitly and are computationally intensive to train.

The interpretability of ML models for physical discovery constitutes
a parallel active frontier.  Symbolic regression (SR)
methods such as SINDy~\cite{Brunton2016}, Eureqa~\cite{Schmidt2009},
and PySR~\cite{Cranmer2020} have demonstrated that compact,
human-readable governing equations can be recovered from data, and
recent benchmarks~\cite{LaCava2021,Angelis2023} have shown their
maturity across physical domains.  However, their application to
aerodynamic shock physics at industrial scale, and in particular
their use \emph{inside} a deployed surrogate as a functional
component rather than a post-hoc explanation, has not been
demonstrated.

This work addresses both gaps with \emph{AeroSurrogate}, an
architecture that predicts the four surface aerodynamic coefficients
$[C_p, C_{fx}, C_{fy}, C_{fz}]$ at every mesh node of a transonic
transport configuration from surface geometry and flight condition
alone.  The model reported here is AeroSurrogate, a
$4.6\times 10^{6}$-parameter spatial network; the symbolic shock
criterion of Section~\ref{sec:sr} is a result about its point-wise
predecessor, and is labelled as such throughout, while
Section~\ref{sec:srspatial} reports what the same distillation yields
when it is turned on the spatial indicator.  The four main contributions
are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A \emph{spatial backbone}: an eight-layer message-passing graph
        neural network over a per-simulation $k$-nearest-neighbour
        graph of the surface ($k=8$), which replaces the point-wise
        input vector of the point-wise model and lifts $R^{2}(C_p)$ from $0.9506$ to
        $0.9729$ and $R^{2}(C_{fy})$ from $0.8463$ to $0.9027$; the two
        sets of scores come from different test protocols, compared in
        Section~\ref{sec:results}.
  \item A \emph{shock-gated Mixture of Experts} in which four smooth
        experts specialise over the surface and a dedicated shock
        expert contributes an additive residual scaled by a learned
        shock probability, an explicit architectural mechanism for the
        pressure discontinuity that single smooth networks cannot
        represent.  Ablating that residual at inference triples the
        $C_p$ mean absolute error on shock nodes, from $0.0458$ to
        $0.1370$.
  \item A \emph{ShockIndicator} network that reads the backbone latent,
        so that shock detection is a neighbourhood rather than a
        point-wise operation, supervised exclusively on labels derived
        from real RANS surface pressures via the critical pressure
        coefficient criterion (requiring no solver at inference and no
        isentropic pseudo-labels at training) and reaching
        $F_1 = 0.9421$ against those labels over eight held-out
        simulations.
  \item A \emph{two-stage training schedule}: end-to-end pre-training
        followed by a head-only fine-tune with the backbone frozen.
        This outperforms joint training from scratch by
        $+0.0149$ in global $R^{2}(C_p)$ and by $+0.0394$ inside the
        shock region on the held-out set, on a single run per arm.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:dataset} describes the simulation database, the
16-dimensional feature vector, and the physics-based shock labelling.
Section~\ref{sec:method} presents the AeroSurrogate architecture, the
routing mechanism, and the training procedure.
Section~\ref{sec:sr} details the symbolic regression stage and the
symbolic-gate retraining carried out for the point-wise model, and
the distillation of the spatial indicator into the same feature
space.
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports results on the held-out test set, and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} draws conclusions.

\section{DATASET AND PROBLEM FORMULATION}
\label{sec:dataset}
% ================================================================

\subsection{Configuration and simulation database}

The ONERA Common Research Model Wing--Body--Pylon--Nacelle
(CRM~WBPN)~\cite{Vassberg2008,Levy2013} is a high-fidelity
industry-standard benchmark for transonic transport aerodynamics.
This work builds on the CRM WBPN machine-learning database recently
released by ONERA~\cite{Peter2025}: 468 steady RANS simulations
computed with the elsA solver on a 39.5M-cell structured Chimera
mesh, using the Spalart--Allmaras turbulence model with quadratic
constitutive relation (QCR) and fixed transition (imposed laminar
zones up to 10\% chord on the wing).  The design of experiments
spans 13 freestream Mach numbers $M \in [0.30,\,0.96]$, twelve
angles of attack per Mach number within
$\alpha \in [-15^{\circ},\,+15^{\circ}]$ (with progressively narrower
ranges at the highest Mach numbers), and three stagnation pressures
$\Pi \in \{1,\,2,\,4\}\times 10^{5}$~Pa covering the Reynolds-number
envelope.  Surface data (pressure coefficient $C_p$ and
skin-friction components $C_{fx}$, $C_{fy}$, $C_{fz}$) are provided
at $n_p = 260\,774$ wall points per condition, i.e.\ roughly 122
million surface points in total.  The dataset is partitioned
\emph{at the simulation level} following the ONERA regression
challenge: 312 conditions ($\approx$81.4M points) for training and
156 held-out conditions ($\approx$40.7M points) for testing, so that
no flight condition seen during training appears in the test set.
All 312 training-partition conditions are used for training: the
sixteen simulations on which early stopping selects the checkpoint
are drawn from the test partition instead
(Section~\ref{sec:training}).  Those sixteen are consequently
excluded from every test metric reported in this paper, which is
computed over the remaining 140 simulations, comprising 36\,508\,360
surface points that influenced neither training nor model selection.
The prediction target at each surface point is
$\mathbf{y} = [C_p,\,C_{fx},\,C_{fy},\,C_{fz}] \in \mathbb{R}^{4}$;
the node features and the targets are standardised (z-score) with
training-set statistics, while the edge features introduced below
reach the network unnormalised, in physical units.

\subsection{Feature engineering}

Each surface point is characterised by nine base features:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{base}}
  = \bigl[x,\;y,\;z,\;n_x,\;n_y,\;n_z,\;M,\;\alpha,\;
          \Pi{\times}10^{-5}\bigr]
  \in \mathbb{R}^{9}
  \label{eq:xbase}
\end{equation}
where $(x,y,z)$ are surface coordinates normalised by the reference
chord, $(n_x,n_y,n_z)$ is the outward unit normal, $M$ is the
freestream Mach number, $\alpha$ is the angle of attack in degrees,
and $\Pi$ is the freestream total pressure.  Five physics-derived
features are appended, each computed analytically from
$\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{base}}$ alone:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    q_{\mathrm{dyn}} &= \frac{M^{2}}{2}, \\
    \Pi_{\mathrm{norm}} &= \frac{\Pi}{1+\tfrac{\gamma-1}{2}M^{2}}, \\
    \mathrm{AoA}_{\sin} &= \sin\alpha
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:derived1}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    L_{\mathrm{factor}} &=
      \frac{\sqrt{1-M^{2}}}{1+\tfrac{\gamma-1}{2}M^{2}}, \\
    C_{p}^{\mathrm{crit}}(M) &=
      \frac{2}{\gamma M^{2}}\!\left[
        \left(\frac{2}{\gamma+1}+\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma+1}M^{2}\right)^{\!\!
          \gamma/(\gamma-1)}\!\!\!-\,1\right]
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:cpcrit}
\end{equation}
where $\gamma = 1.4$ for air.  Here $\Pi_{\mathrm{norm}}$ normalises
the total pressure by the isentropic temperature ratio, making the
feature independent of the absolute pressure level; $L_{\mathrm{factor}}$
combines the Prandtl--Glauert compressibility factor $\sqrt{1-M^{2}}$
with the same denominator, providing a Laitone-type compressibility
scale that goes to zero at the sonic limit.  The quantity $C_{p}^{\mathrm{crit}}(M)$ is the
pressure coefficient at which the local flow reaches the sonic
condition ($M_{\mathrm{local}}=1$) under isentropic assumptions; it
is the key physical threshold used for shock labelling.  Finally, two
geometry features are computed from the spatial statistics of the
mesh: the chord-wise position $x_{\mathrm{norm}}\in[0,1]$ (0 at the
leading edge, 1 at the trailing edge) and the span-wise position
$\eta = \mathrm{span}_{\mathrm{norm}}\in[0,1]$ (0 at the root, 1 at
the tip).  The full per-node input vector is therefore
$\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{16}$; all derived features require only
$\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{base}}$.  No solver output enters at the
feature-engineering stage.  Table~\ref{tab:features} lists all
sixteen node features with their physical interpretation.

The two geometry features are consistent with the feature-importance
analysis of Section~\ref{sec:srresults}, in which chord-wise and
span-wise position rank as the two most informative variables for
shock localisation: the shock on a transonic transport wing sits at a
characteristic chord fraction and strengthens over the mid-span
region, so providing these coordinates explicitly relieves the
network from having to infer them from raw $(x,y,z)$.

The sixteen node features are not the whole input.  Because the present model treats
a simulation as a graph rather than as a bag of independent points, a
$k$-nearest-neighbour graph over its $n_p$ surface points is derived
from the same raw columns: the neighbour relation is computed from
the coordinates alone, and each of the resulting $E = k\,n_p$
directed edges carries five features built from the coordinates and
the normals of its two endpoints.  With $k=8$ this adds
$E = 2\,086\,192$ edges per simulation.  The construction is given in
Section~\ref{sec:backbone}; like the node features it uses no solver
output, and because only the point coordinates enter it, no mesh
connectivity is required and a bare surface point cloud suffices.

\subsection{Shock label generation from RANS output}

A binary shock label is defined for each surface point using the RANS
solver output:
\begin{equation}
  y_{\mathrm{shock}} =
    \mathbf{1}\!\left[C_{p}^{\mathrm{RANS}} <
                      C_{p}^{\mathrm{crit}}(M)\right]
    \wedge
    \mathbf{1}\!\left[M > 0.75\right]
  \label{eq:labels}
\end{equation}
The first factor identifies surface points where the RANS-predicted
pressure coefficient falls below the sonic threshold.  That is the
condition for a locally supersonic flow above the boundary layer,
i.e.\ the footprint of the supersonic pocket terminated by the shock.
The second restricts the label to the transonic part of the envelope:
below a freestream Mach number of $0.75$ an excursion past the
critical value is not the wing shock the model is asked to capture,
so such points are labelled negative and supervised as negatives.
The same $M > 0.75$ threshold separates the \emph{transonic} and
\emph{subsonic} subsets on which results are reported in
Section~\ref{sec:results}, and the \emph{shock} subset used there is
the transonic points that additionally satisfy
$C_{p}^{\mathrm{RANS}} < C_{p}^{\mathrm{crit}}(M)$.  Critically,
$C_{p}^{\mathrm{RANS}}$ is a solver output embedding viscous,
turbulent, and compressibility effects; the threshold
$C_{p}^{\mathrm{crit}}$ is a well-established physical criterion, not
a model assumption applied to the inputs.  This distinguishes the
present labelling strategy from pseudo-label approaches that derive
shock indicators directly from the input
features~\cite{Ray2018,Discacciati2020}, which would render the
learning problem circular.  The labels are used \emph{only during
training}; at inference the model receives the node features and the
derived graph alone.  Under this definition $18.7\%$ of the surface
points of the held-out partition carry a positive shock label
(6\,830\,670 of 36\,508\,360).

\section{METHODOLOGY: THE AEROSURROGATE ARCHITECTURE}
\label{sec:method}
% ================================================================

AeroSurrogate is a single end-to-end trainable model with a spatial
backbone and three prediction heads.  An eight-layer message-passing
GNN over a per-simulation $k$-nearest-neighbour graph of the surface
($k=8$, Section~\ref{sec:backbone}) maps each node to a
256-dimensional latent $\mathbf{h}_i$; a
\emph{ShockIndicator} estimates from $\mathbf{h}_i$ the probability
$p_{s,i}$ that the node lies inside the supersonic pocket; a
\emph{shock-gated Mixture of Experts} (ShockGatedMoE) predicts the
pressure coefficient $C_p$ from $[\mathbf{h}_i\,\|\,p_{s,i}]$; and a
separate \emph{FrictionHead} predicts the friction vector
$(C_{fx},C_{fy},C_{fz})$ from $\mathbf{h}_i$ as a magnitude and a unit
direction.  Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} shows the complete
architecture and data flow.  The total parameter count is
4\,603\,918, of which 3\,472\,640 ($75.4\%$) lie in the GNN backbone,
43\,713 in the ShockIndicator, 755\,529 in the Mixture of Experts and
332\,036 in the FrictionHead.  At inference the model requires the
surface point cloud and the flight condition (from which the kNN
graph and all node and edge features are derived analytically) but no
CFD solver output.

\subsection{Spatial backbone}
\label{sec:backbone}

The backbone is the component that distinguishes AeroSurrogate from its
point-wise predecessor, and it carries $75.4\%$ of the parameters.

The \textbf{graph} is built once per simulation.  Its $N =
260\,774$ nodes are the surface mesh points, each carrying the
sixteen-dimensional feature vector $\mathbf{x}_i$ of
Table~\ref{tab:features}.  Its edges are the $k$-nearest-neighbour
relation of the node coordinates, computed with a KD-tree and with
self-edges excluded; with $k=8$ this gives $E = 8N = 2\,086\,192$
directed edges $(i \to j)$ for $j \in \mathrm{kNN}(i)$.  Only the
coordinates enter the construction, so no mesh connectivity is
required and a bare point cloud suffices.  Each edge carries the
five-dimensional feature
$\mathbf{e}_{ij} = (\delta x, \delta y, \delta z,
\|\boldsymbol{\delta}\|, \mathbf{n}_i\!\cdot\!\mathbf{n}_j)$, where
$\boldsymbol{\delta}$ is the displacement from $i$ to $j$ and
$\mathbf{n}$ the unit surface normal, so that the network is given
both the local metric and the local curvature of the surface.

\textbf{Message passing} proceeds as follows.  A node encoder
$16\!\to\!128\!\to\!256$ lifts $\mathbf{x}_i$ to a latent
$\mathbf{h}_i \in \mathbb{R}^{256}$ and an edge encoder
$5\!\to\!64\!\to\!128$ lifts $\mathbf{e}_{ij}$.  Each of the eight
message-passing layers then computes a message at the source of every
edge, aggregates the incoming messages at the destination by
concatenated mean and maximum, and applies a residual update
normalised by LayerNorm:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{m}_{i\to j} =
    \phi_{m}\bigl([\mathbf{h}_i \,\|\, \mathbf{e}_{ij}]\bigr),
  \qquad
  \mathbf{h}_j \leftarrow
    \mathrm{LN}\Bigl(\mathbf{h}_j + \phi_{u}\bigl([\mathbf{h}_j \,\|\,
      \mathrm{mean}\,\mathbf{m} \,\|\, \mathrm{max}\,\mathbf{m}]\bigr)\Bigr)
  \label{eq:backbone}
\end{equation}
with $\phi_m: 384\!\to\!256\!\to\!256$ and
$\phi_u: 768\!\to\!256\!\to\!256$.  The relation is deliberately
\emph{not} symmetrised: messages are emitted at the source and
scattered to the destination, so node $j$ pools over its reverse-kNN
neighbourhood, whose size varies across the surface because
$k$-nearest-neighbour adjacency is not a symmetric relation.  Dense
regions of the mesh therefore aggregate over more incoming edges than
sparse ones, which is the behaviour wanted where the mesh is refined
around the shock.

The \textbf{depth} of eight layers gives an eight-hop
receptive field, so each latent summarises a patch of surface rather
than a point.  The residual connection and LayerNorm on every layer
are what make that depth usable: without them, eight successive rounds
of mean/max aggregation collapse the latents towards a common value.
Storing the activations of eight layers over a graph of this size does
not fit in device memory, so the backbone is trained with activation
checkpointing, trading one extra forward pass per layer during the
backward pass for activation memory independent of depth.  This is
what allows a whole simulation to be a single optimiser step.
Checkpointing is disabled when the backbone is frozen, where the
recomputation would be pure overhead.

\subsection{ShockIndicator}
\label{sec:indicator}

The ShockIndicator is an MLP with layer sizes
$256\!\to\!128\!\to\!64\!\to\!32\!\to\!1$; every hidden layer applies
LayerNorm~\cite{Ba2016}, SiLU activation~\cite{Elfwing2018}, and
dropout (rate 0.1), and the final layer is linear:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    \mathrm{shock\_logit}_i &=
      f_{\mathrm{SI}}(\mathbf{h}_i;\theta_{\mathrm{SI}}) \in \mathbb{R}, \\
    p_{s,i} &= \sigma(\mathrm{shock\_logit}_i) \in [0,1]
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:indicator}
\end{equation}
Crucially, the indicator no longer reads the raw point features but
the backbone latent $\mathbf{h}_i$, which after eight rounds of
message passing already summarises an eight-hop neighbourhood.  The
sensor is therefore \emph{spatial}: it can condition on the local
pressure-gradient structure that defines a shock, and not only on the
point's own state.

It is supervised by the RANS-derived label $y_{\mathrm{shock}}$
through a binary cross-entropy term (Section~\ref{sec:loss}).  Note
that this label is a conjunction, so $p_s$ estimates the probability
that a point lies in a supersonic pocket \emph{and} that the
free-stream is transonic; it is trained to read zero on a genuinely
supersonic pocket occurring at $M \le 0.75$, and is therefore a
detector of the transonic shock regime rather than of local
supersonic flow as such.  Measured against that label on eight
held-out test simulations, the reported model attains precision
$0.8964$, recall $0.9927$ and $F_1 = 0.9421$, with a mean $p_s$ of
$0.9765$ on labelled shock nodes against $0.0317$ elsewhere.

The indicator is trained inside the surrogate rather than as a
separate pretraining stage, but the reported model is nevertheless
produced by a two-stage schedule: end-to-end training followed by a
head-only fine-tune with the backbone frozen
(Section~\ref{sec:training}).  The scalar $p_s$ serves simultaneously
as (i)~an input to the routing gate, (ii)~the multiplicative gain of
the shock expert, and (iii)~an interpretable shock-detection output in
its own right.  It acts on the $C_p$ branch only: the friction head of
Section~\ref{sec:friction} is not gated by it.

\subsection{Shock-gated Mixture of Experts}
\label{sec:moe}

The ShockGatedMoE comprises a gating network, $K=4$ smooth experts and
one shock expert, and predicts the pressure coefficient alone; the
friction vector is produced by a separate head
(Section~\ref{sec:friction}).  The \textbf{gate} receives the
concatenation $[\mathbf{h}_i\,\|\,p_{s,i}]\in\mathbb{R}^{257}$ and
maps it through $257\!\to\!128\!\to\!64\!\to\!4$ (LayerNorm + SiLU on
the hidden layers, no dropout) to $K$ routing logits $\mathbf{l}_i$.
Routing is therefore conditioned on the node's aggregated spatial
context (which already encodes the flight condition, the chord-wise
and span-wise station and the neighbourhood pressure
structure) together with the estimated shock state.  Each
\textbf{smooth expert} is an MLP
$256\!\to\!256\!\to\!256\!\to\!128\!\to\!1$ (LayerNorm + SiLU +
dropout).

Because the point-wise gate saw only the point's own feature vector, its
experts could specialise at most per flight condition.  The present gate is
per node, so any specialisation it develops is spatial, and it has
been measured rather than assumed.  Over eight held-out test
simulations the mean gate weight of expert $E_0$ rises from $0.1451$
off shock to $0.4722$ on labelled shock nodes, while $E_2$ falls from
$0.3144$ to $0.0631$; the total variation between the two
distributions is $0.3581$.  The specialisation emerges from routing
rather than from explicit labels, as in classical and sparse mixtures
of experts~\cite{Jacobs1991,Shazeer2017}.  We do not claim a partition
of the flight envelope into named regimes: no such partition has been
measured.  The value $K=4$ is inherited unchanged from the point-wise
predecessor and was not re-tuned here; a sweep over $K$ is left to
future work.

The \textbf{shock expert} is a smaller MLP
$256\!\to\!128\!\to\!128\!\to\!1$ whose output enters the prediction as
an additive residual scaled by $p_s$, correcting $C_p$ only:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{C}_{p,i} =
    \underbrace{\sum_{k=1}^{K} g_{k}(\mathbf{h}_i, p_{s,i})\,
                f_{k}(\mathbf{h}_i)}_{\text{smooth MoE output}}
    \;+\;
    \underbrace{p_{s,i}\, f^{\mathrm{shock}}(\mathbf{h}_i)}_{\text{shock residual}}
  \label{eq:forward_cp}
\end{equation}

The shock residual is the key architectural innovation.  A smooth
decoder over the spatial latent can only approximate a discontinuous
function by smearing it.  The smoothing pathology now sits downstream
of the eight-layer backbone rather than on raw point features, but it
is not removed by it.  Assigning one of the standard experts to
shocked flow does not help either, since that expert would still need
to be simultaneously differentiable and sharply discontinuous.  The
residual formulation instead mirrors the physics: the total loading is
decomposed into a smooth background plus a shock perturbation.  In
subsonic flow $p_s\!\approx\!0$ and the correction vanishes
identically; inside the supersonic pocket $p_s\!\approx\!1$ and the
shock expert supplies the learned pressure-jump correction.  The
transition sharpness is inherited from the sigmoid of the
ShockIndicator rather than being limited by the smoothness of the
regression experts.  The construction deliberately parallels
classical shock-capturing practice in numerical schemes, where
localised artificial dissipation is activated only near
discontinuities flagged by a sensor~\cite{Discacciati2020,
Schwander2021}: here both the sensor ($p_s$) and the local
correction ($f^{\mathrm{shock}}$) are learned, but the design
principle of separating the treatment of the discontinuity from the
smooth approximation is the same.

The contribution of the residual can be measured directly by deleting
the term at inference from an otherwise unchanged model.  On the
labelled shock nodes of eight held-out test simulations this triples
the $C_p$ mean absolute error, from $0.0458$ to $0.1370$; equivalently,
the residual removes $66.6\%$ of the error the model would otherwise
incur there.  On those same nodes the residual carries $43.4\%$ of the
magnitude of the smooth term.

\subsection{Friction head}
\label{sec:friction}

The friction vector is predicted by a head that reads $\mathbf{h}_i$
in parallel with the $C_p$ branch and factorises the prediction into a
magnitude and a unit direction.  A magnitude branch
$256\!\to\!256\!\to\!256\!\to\!128\!\to\!1$ produces a log-magnitude,
which is clipped to $[-10,5]$ and exponentiated; a direction branch
$256\!\to\!256\!\to\!256\!\to\!128\!\to\!3$ produces an unnormalised
direction, which is $L_2$-normalised:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    \hat{\mathbf{C}}_{f,i} &=
      \exp\!\bigl(\mathrm{clip}(f_{\mathrm{mag}}(\mathbf{h}_i),\,-10,\,5)\bigr)\;
      \frac{f_{\mathrm{dir}}(\mathbf{h}_i)}{\|f_{\mathrm{dir}}(\mathbf{h}_i)\|}, \\
    \hat{\mathbf{y}}_i &= \bigl[\hat{C}_{p,i},\;\hat{\mathbf{C}}_{f,i}\bigr]
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:forward_cf}
\end{equation}
The factorisation is motivated by the two quantities behaving very
differently.  Skin-friction magnitude spans several orders of
magnitude over the surface, from the stagnation region to the
separated wake, which is why it is learned in the logarithm; the
direction, by contrast, is a bounded quantity constrained to the local
tangent plane, and its errors are best penalised by an angle.  The two
are supervised separately by the two halves of
Eq.~(\ref{eq:fric}).  Replacing the point-wise model's shared four-output experts with
this dedicated head, together with the spatial backbone, is what lifts
$R^{2}(C_{fy})$ from $0.8463$ to $0.9027$.

Two properties of the head are worth stating explicitly because the
architecture diagram could otherwise suggest the opposite.  First, it
is \emph{not} gated by $p_s$ and does not see the gate weights: it
consumes the backbone latent alone, so the explicit discontinuity
mechanism acts on the pressure field only.  Second, at 332\,036
parameters it accounts for $7.2\%$ of the model.

\subsection{Gumbel-Softmax routing}
\label{sec:gumbel}

Hard (argmax) expert selection would make the gradients of all
non-selected experts vanish and collapse training.  During training
the gate therefore uses the Gumbel-Softmax
relaxation~\cite{Jang2017,Maddison2017}, which provides
differentiable, near-one-hot routing as the temperature $\tau$ is
annealed.  The relaxation is applied to \emph{every} node whenever the
model is in training mode; the branch is on the mode, not on the flow
regime:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    \mathbf{g}_i &=
    \begin{cases}
      \mathrm{softmax}\!\bigl((\mathbf{l}_i+\mathbf{G}_i)/\tau\bigr) &
        \text{training (all nodes)}\\[2pt]
      \mathrm{softmax}(\mathbf{l}_i/\tau)       & \text{inference}
    \end{cases} \\[4pt]
    G_k &= -\log(-\log u_k),\;\; u_k \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1)
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:routing}
\end{equation}
where the $G_k$ are i.i.d.\ samples of the standard Gumbel
distribution.  Perturbing the logits with Gumbel noise before the
tempered softmax is equivalent to sampling an expert from the
categorical distribution defined by $\mathbf{l}_i$ as $\tau \to 0$,
while keeping the routing differentiable with respect to the gate
parameters at every finite temperature.  This is precisely the
reparameterisation trick for discrete
variables~\cite{Jang2017,Maddison2017}.  The straight-through variant
is not used: the relaxed weights themselves multiply the expert
outputs, so the mixture stays soft throughout training.  The
temperature is annealed \emph{linearly} in the epoch index,
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    \tau(t) &= \tau_{0} + \frac{t}{T-1}\,(\tau_{T}-\tau_{0}), \\
    \tau_{0} &= 1.0,\quad \tau_{T}=0.3,\quad T=200,
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:tau}
\end{equation}
and the schedule restarts at $\tau_0$ for the second, head-only
training stage.  At inference the gate uses a deterministic softmax at
the temperature stored in the selected checkpoint.  Because
checkpoints are written at the best validation epoch rather than at
the end of the schedule, the reported model operates at
$\tau = 0.5884$ rather than at $\tau_T$.  The routing it produces is
nevertheless close to one-hot: the mean per-node gate entropy measured
over eight held-out test simulations is $0.080$, against a maximum of
$\log K = 1.3863$.

\subsection{Normalisation and activation choices}
\label{sec:norm}

All hidden layers in AeroSurrogate use LayerNorm and SiLU, a
deliberate departure from the common BatchNorm + ReLU recipe.
BatchNorm computes batch statistics during training but applies
running averages at inference; with the strongly heterogeneous data
distribution across flight conditions this introduced a systematic
train/evaluation gap in early experiments.  LayerNorm normalises each
sample over its feature dimensions and therefore behaves identically
at training and inference, eliminating the gap entirely.  SiLU
($x\,\sigma(x)$)~\cite{Elfwing2018,Ramachandran2017} is smooth and
avoids the dead-neuron pathology that ReLU can exhibit in combination
with LayerNorm, and consistently outperformed ReLU-family activations
on this regression task.  In addition, every message-passing layer
applies a LayerNorm to its residual sum, Eq.~(\ref{eq:backbone}); this
pairing of residual connection and normalisation is what keeps eight
successive rounds of mean/max aggregation from over-smoothing the
latents.  Dropout (rate 0.1) is applied in every MLP block of the
model with a single exception: the gating network is built without it,
the Gumbel relaxation of Section~\ref{sec:gumbel} already supplying
stochasticity on the routing path.

\subsection{Loss function}
\label{sec:loss}

Training minimises a four-term objective, evaluated over the $N$ nodes
of one simulation graph:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
    \mathcal{L} ={}&
      w_f\underbrace{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i\,
        \bigl(\hat{C}_{p,i} - C_{p,i}\bigr)^{2}}_{\text{shock-weighted }C_p\text{ MSE}}
      \;+\; \lambda_{s}\,
      \underbrace{\mathrm{BCE}\bigl(\mathrm{shock\_logit},\,
        y_{\mathrm{shock}}\bigr)}_{\text{indicator supervision}} \\[2ex]
    &\;-\; \lambda_{\mathrm{lb}}\,
      \underbrace{H(\bar{\mathbf{g}})}_{\text{load balance}}
      \;+\; \lambda_{\mathrm{fric}}\,w_f\,
      \underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fric}}}_{\text{friction}}
  \end{split}
  \label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
where the load-balance and friction terms are
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    H(\bar{\mathbf{g}}) &= -\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bar{g}_k\log\bar{g}_k, \\
    \bar{g}_k &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} g_{i,k}
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:lb}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fric}} =
    \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
      \bigl(\log\|\hat{\mathbf{C}}_{f,i}\| -
            \log\|\mathbf{C}_{f,i}\|\bigr)^{2}
    \;+\;
    \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
      \min\!\left(\frac{\|\mathbf{C}_{f,i}\|}
                       {\overline{\|\mathbf{C}_{f}\|}},\,5\right)
      \bigl(1-\cos(\hat{\mathbf{C}}_{f,i},\mathbf{C}_{f,i})\bigr)
  \label{eq:fric}
\end{equation}
and the weights are
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    w_i &= 1 + \lambda_{\mathrm{sw}}\,y_{\mathrm{shock},i}, \\
    \lambda_{s} &= 0.1,\quad \lambda_{\mathrm{lb}}=0.01,\quad
      \lambda_{\mathrm{sw}}=5.0,\quad \lambda_{\mathrm{fric}}=3.0
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:weights}
\end{equation}
Here $w_f$ is the per-simulation confidence weight
\texttt{confidence\_weight\_simple} shipped with the database, which
takes exactly two values, $1.0$ and $0.5$, the latter at the extreme
incidences where the reference solutions are least converged.  It is
the ONERA regression-challenge weighting, which is what makes the
weighted $R^{2}$ reported in Section~\ref{sec:results} comparable with
the challenge reference results.  Both $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fric}}$
terms are evaluated after affine denormalisation to physical units.

The \emph{shock-weighted $C_p$ MSE} assigns $6\times$ loss weight to
points carrying a positive shock label.  Because that label itself
conjoins $C_p < C_p^{\mathrm{crit}}$ with $M > 0.75$, subsonic points
whose $C_p$ dips below the critical value (physically ambiguous at
low Mach) are labelled negative, and are therefore both weighted at
unity and supervised as negatives by the BCE term; no separate Mach
indicator is needed in $w_i$.  The reweighting applies to the $C_p$
term alone: the friction objective carries the per-simulation weight
$w_f$ but never the node weight $w_i$, so shock nodes receive no
special treatment in the friction branch.  The \emph{BCE term}
supervises the ShockIndicator with positive-class weight 5.0 to
compensate for label imbalance; under the present label the measured
positive rate is $21.0\%$ (438\,984 of 2\,086\,192 nodes over eight
test simulations).  Neither it nor the load-balance term is multiplied
by $w_f$, which scales the two regression terms alone.

The \emph{load-balance term} is the entropy $H(\bar{\mathbf{g}})$ of
the batch-averaged gate distribution, Eq.~(\ref{eq:lb}), which is
\emph{maximised} (that is, subtracted from the minimised objective).
It attains its maximum $\log K = 1.3863$ under perfectly uniform
routing and vanishes when the gate collapses onto a single expert.  It
plays the role of the auxiliary load-balancing losses used in sparse
mixtures of experts~\cite{Shazeer2017,Fedus2022}, but is not the same
functional form: those penalise a squared usage moment, whereas
Eq.~(\ref{eq:lb}) rewards the entropy directly.  On the reported model
the term settles at $1.3827$, $99.7\%$ of uniform, with no expert
falling below $1\%$ of the routing mass.  The sign of this term is not
a cosmetic detail: adding the entropy to a minimised objective instead
of subtracting it drives the gate towards collapse rather than away
from it, and an earlier run trained that way collapsed onto a single
expert (Section~\ref{sec:results}).  Finally, the \emph{friction
term}, Eq.~(\ref{eq:fric}), separates a log-magnitude mean-squared
error from a cosine direction error, the latter down-weighted where
the true friction vector is small and its direction correspondingly
ill-conditioned.

\subsection{Training procedure}
\label{sec:training}

The complete workflow consists of four steps
(Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}): (1)~\emph{preprocessing}, in which the
seven physics and geometry features are derived from the nine raw
columns and the z-score scalers and chord/span bounds are fitted on
the training split; (2)~\emph{graph construction}, in which a $k=8$
kNN graph per simulation is built with a KD-tree over the node
coordinates, annotated with the five-dimensional edge features and
cached to disk for all 468 simulations; (3)~\emph{stage~A}, end-to-end
training of the whole model from scratch; and (4)~\emph{stage~B}, a
head-only fine-tune resuming from that checkpoint with the backbone
frozen, which is the reported model.  All four terms of
Eq.~(\ref{eq:loss}) are active in both training stages, and the
temperature schedule of Eq.~(\ref{eq:tau}) restarts at $\tau_0$ at the
beginning of stage~B.  Freezing the backbone in stage~B holds the
3\,472\,640 backbone parameters ($75.4\%$ of the model) fixed, so
only the heads are updated.  Measured on the held-out test set,
the two-stage schedule improves the global $R^{2}(C_p)$ by $0.0149$
and the shock-region $R^{2}(C_p)$ by $0.0394$ over joint end-to-end
training from scratch under an otherwise identical budget, loss and
data; this comparison rests on one run per arm, so seed variance
cannot be excluded.

Optimisation uses AdamW ($\beta_1=0.9$, $\beta_2=0.999$) with a peak
learning rate of $10^{-3}$ reached after three epochs of linear warmup
and then cosine-annealed to $10^{-6}$, weight decay $10^{-5}$,
gradient-norm clipping at 1.0, and one full simulation graph
(260\,774 nodes, 2\,086\,192 edges) per optimiser step.  Each stage
runs for up to 200 epochs, with validation every two epochs and early
stopping after eight consecutive non-improving checks; the reported
model reaches its best validation score at epoch 118 and stops on
patience at epoch 134.  Checkpoints are selected on the
\emph{unweighted} $R^{2}(C_p)$ over a fixed subset of 16 validation
simulations, so that selection is biased neither by the training-time
shock reweighting nor by the per-simulation confidence weights.

Those 16 validation simulations are drawn from the \emph{test} split,
so early stopping does see test simulations.  Every accuracy figure
reported in Section~\ref{sec:results} is therefore computed on the
remaining 140 simulations (36\,508\,360 surface points), which never
influenced model selection.  The point-wise baseline figures are quoted from
their own protocol: a 10\% random sample of all 156 test
simulations, 4\,068\,074 points.  The point-wise and present-model
columns of Table~\ref{tab:results} are therefore not measured on the same
population.  The point-wise model did, however, select its checkpoint
on a slice of the training partition, so its test simulations played
no part in its own model selection.

\subsection{Inference pipeline}
\label{sec:inference}

At inference, no CFD solver output is required.  The deployed model
consumes three sources only: the surface mesh nodes
$(x,y,z,n_x,n_y,n_z)$, available directly from the CAD or mesh file;
the flight condition $(M,\alpha,\Pi)$, taken from the test matrix or
flight envelope; and two one-time training artefacts, namely the
z-score statistics and the chord/span bounds fitted once on the
training mesh.  Unlike the point-wise predecessor, however, AeroSurrogate
consumes the surface \emph{as a whole}: the $k=8$ neighbour graph is
rebuilt from the node coordinates alone (no mesh connectivity is
required, so a bare point cloud suffices) and the five edge features
follow from those coordinates and the surface normals.  The model
therefore cannot score an isolated point or an arbitrary subset of the
surface, which is a genuine deployment regression relative to the
point-wise formulation.  Prediction proceeds in five steps:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Analytical feature construction}: the five physics
        features are evaluated from the closed-form isentropic
        relations of Eqs.~(\ref{eq:derived1})--(\ref{eq:cpcrit}), and
        the two geometry features $x_{\mathrm{norm}}$ and $\eta$ are
        linear rescalings of the mesh coordinates using the stored
        bounds, yielding $\mathbf{x}_i\in\mathbb{R}^{16}$ per node with
        no solver call.
  \item \emph{Graph construction}: a $k=8$ nearest-neighbour graph is
        built over the node coordinates with a KD-tree (self-edges
        excluded), giving $E=8N$ directed edges, each annotated with
        $(\delta x,\delta y,\delta z,\|\boldsymbol{\delta}\|,
        \mathbf{n}_i\!\cdot\!\mathbf{n}_j)$.  This costs approximately
        1.5~s per 260\,774-node surface and is cached, so it is paid
        once per geometry rather than once per flight condition.
  \item \emph{Normalisation} of the node features $\mathbf{x}_i$ with
        the stored training statistics.  The edge features are passed
        to the edge encoder unnormalised, in physical units.
  \item \emph{A single forward pass over the whole graph}, producing
        $\hat{C}_p$ from the shock-gated mixture
        (Eq.~(\ref{eq:forward_cp})) and
        $[\hat{C}_{fx},\hat{C}_{fy},\hat{C}_{fz}]$ from the friction
        head (Eq.~(\ref{eq:forward_cf})), together with the per-node
        shock probability $p_{s,i}$ and the gate weights as
        interpretable by-products.
  \item \emph{Denormalisation} of
        $[\hat{C}_p,\hat{C}_{fx},\hat{C}_{fy},\hat{C}_{fz}]$ to
        physical units.
\end{enumerate}
The complete pipeline comprises feature construction, a graph build and
one forward pass.  It requires no flow solver at any stage, against
$\mathcal{O}(10^{3}$--$10^{4})$ CPU-hours for a RANS solution of a
single flight condition, and only surface-level inputs that are
equally available in experimental or flight-test contexts.  Its
dominant cost is the eight rounds of message passing over the
$2\,086\,192$ edges of the graph.  A like-for-like wall-clock timing
against the solver and against the point-wise predecessor has not been
carried out for the present model and is left to future work; the unit of work is now
an entire surface rather than a batch of independent points, so the
two are not directly comparable.

\section{SYMBOLIC SHOCK CRITERIA: THE POINT-WISE MODEL AND THE
  SPATIAL INDICATOR}
\label{sec:sr}
% ================================================================

This section reports two symbolic results and keeps them apart
throughout.  The first concerns the \emph{point-wise predecessor} of
the model described above, the point-wise baseline quoted in
Table~\ref{tab:results}.  It admits a closed-form shock criterion
because its shock indicator reads a vector of sixteen named physical
variables, nine of which are offered to the search
(Sections~\ref{sec:srresults} and~\ref{sec:symgate}).  The
second turns the same machinery on the spatial indicator of the
present model, which reads the latent produced by the message-passing
backbone.  There the nine variables are no longer the indicator's own
inputs but a candidate basis in which to approximate its output, and
Section~\ref{sec:srspatial} measures how far that approximation gets
and what substituting it costs.

Performed with PySR~\cite{Cranmer2020}, the search serves two
purposes: (i)~\emph{interpretability} (a human-readable equation for
the shock criterion) and (ii)~\emph{physical validation}, whether
the criterion the RANS-derived labels encode is recoverable from a
small set of named aerodynamic variables, and whether the resulting
formula reproduces recognisable transonic behaviour.

\subsection{Setup}

The SR stage regresses the \emph{hard physics label}
$y_{\mathrm{shock}}\in\{0,1\}$ of Eq.~(\ref{eq:labels}), not the
soft output of the neural ShockIndicator, on a subset of nine
physically meaningful features:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{sr}} = [\,M,\;\alpha,\;x_{\mathrm{norm}},\;
  \eta,\; n_{z},\; C_{p}^{\mathrm{crit}},\; q_{\mathrm{dyn}},\;
  \mathrm{AoA}_{\sin},\; L_{\mathrm{factor}}\,]
  \label{eq:srfeatures}
\end{equation}
Raw geometric coordinates $(x,y,z)$ and the normal components
$(n_x,n_y)$ are excluded to bias the search toward conditions based
on flow physics and aerodynamically meaningful coordinates rather
than absolute surface location, and the two total-pressure features
$\Pi$ and $\Pi_{\mathrm{norm}}$ are withheld as well.  PySR performs a
multi-objective evolutionary search (200 iterations, 20 populations of
50 expressions, maximum complexity 20, 50\,000 training samples) over
expression trees built from the operator set
$\{+,\,-,\,\times,\,\div,\,\exp,\,\log,\,|\cdot|,\,\tanh\}$, minimising the MSE
penalised by expression complexity (node count), and returns a Pareto
front of candidate expressions.  A RandomForest classifier is trained
in parallel on the same features to provide a model-agnostic feature
importance ranking.

\subsection{Results of the symbolic search}
\label{sec:srresults}

Figure~\ref{fig:importance} reports the RandomForest feature
importances, measured on the same 50\,000-sample training set.  They
rank $x_{\mathrm{norm}}$ ($0.2123$), $\eta$ ($0.1730$), $M$
($0.1275$), $L_{\mathrm{factor}}$ ($0.1176$), $n_{z}$ ($0.0977$),
$C_{p}^{\mathrm{crit}}$ ($0.0965$), $q_{\mathrm{dyn}}$ ($0.0889$),
$\alpha$ ($0.0434$) and $\mathrm{AoA}_{\sin}$ ($0.0431$).  The two
geometric coordinates rank first and second, but the ordering below
them interleaves compressibility and geometry rather than separating
them, and the freestream Mach number is only third.  The reading is
physically plausible: on a transonic transport wing the shock sits at
a characteristic chord fraction ($x/c \approx 0.3$--$0.6$) over the
outer wing, and its occurrence is then modulated by the freestream
Mach number.  The ranking is consistent with the inclusion of
$x_{\mathrm{norm}}$ and $\eta$ in the 16-feature input vector,
although it cannot be said to have motivated it: the classifier was
trained on a feature set that already contained both, and the
symbolic stage runs only after the surrogate has been trained.

From the Pareto front (Figure~\ref{fig:pareto};
Table~\ref{tab:pareto} lists the leading expressions), the
complexity-5 expression with the highest score is selected:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{y}_{\mathrm{shock}}^{\mathrm{SR}}
  = \tanh\!\left(\exp\!\left(
      \frac{C_{p}^{\mathrm{crit}}(M)}{\eta}\right)\right)
  \label{eq:srdiscovered}
\end{equation}
The two leading candidates are not, however, distinguishable as
classifiers.  Complexity~5 differs from complexity~4 only by the
outer $\tanh$, which is strictly monotone, so the two rank every
point identically and share every threshold-free discrimination
measure; the $0.0433$ against $0.0422$ gap is an artefact of
evaluating the mean squared error after the output has been squashed
toward $[0,1]$, not a gain in accuracy.  The $\tanh$ is retained
because it bounds the output, not because it discriminates better.
The front itself keeps descending beyond the selected point
(Figure~\ref{fig:pareto}), so the choice of complexity~5 is one of
legibility rather than of an elbow in the trade-off.

Bounding is not by itself sufficient.  With
$C_{p}^{\mathrm{crit}}(M)<0$ and $\eta\in(0,1]$,
Eq.~(\ref{eq:srdiscovered}) is strictly positive and bounded above by
$\tanh\!\left(\exp\!\left(C_{p}^{\mathrm{crit}}(M)\right)\right) <
\tanh(1) \approx 0.762$, so it never spans the unit interval a
routing probability requires.  What is used as the gate is therefore
the expression composed with a monotone isotonic calibration fitted
on the same labels, with $92$ knots and output range
$[0.0155,\,1.0]$:
\begin{equation}
  p_{s}^{\mathrm{SR}} = \mathrm{Iso}_{92}\!\left(
    \hat{y}_{\mathrm{shock}}^{\mathrm{SR}}\right)
  \label{eq:srcalibrated}
\end{equation}
Being monotone, the calibration changes no ranking; it is a
rescaling that makes the algebraic score usable as a probability, and
it is part of what is deployed.

The expression admits a direct physical reading, and also a clear
limitation.  Since $C_{p}^{\mathrm{crit}}(M)<0$ (the sonic condition
always corresponds to suction) and $\eta\in(0,1]$, the ratio
$C_{p}^{\mathrm{crit}}/\eta$ is negative and increases monotonically
with $\eta$; both $\exp$ and $\tanh$ are increasing, so
$\hat{y}^{\mathrm{SR}}_{\mathrm{shock}}$ is a strictly monotone
inboard-to-outboard ramp whose steepness is set by the Mach number.
Near the root the large-magnitude negative argument drives
$\exp(\cdot)\to 0$ and hence
$\hat{y}^{\mathrm{SR}}_{\mathrm{shock}}\to 0$, which is the correct
behaviour for a region kept unloaded by fuselage interference and
thicker sections.  As the Mach number rises,
$C_{p}^{\mathrm{crit}}$ becomes less negative, the ramp flattens and
the predicted probability rises at every span station, the
near-zero region retreating toward the root.  This is the inboard
spreading of the supersonic pocket with Mach that is characteristic of
swept transonic wings.  Figure~\ref{fig:symmap} maps the expression over
the $(M,\eta)$ plane.

The formula has no interior maximum: it is largest exactly at the tip,
$\eta = 1$.  It therefore does not reproduce tip unloading by wash-out
twist, and places its highest shock probability at the span station
where the local lift coefficient of a swept, tapered transport wing is
in fact lowest.  This is a genuine failure of the recovered criterion
rather than a matter of interpretation, since the monotonicity in
$\eta$ follows from the algebra.  It is the price of a two-variable
expression: the criterion couples the second-ranked factor of the
importance analysis, span-wise station, with the sixth-ranked one,
proximity to the sonic threshold through $C_{p}^{\mathrm{crit}}$ (a
function of Mach only), while omitting the first-ranked chord-wise
coordinate entirely, so it has no means of expressing where the
supersonic pocket terminates.  The quantity this paper can report for
the sensor is accordingly not its standalone discrimination but the
end-to-end cost of substituting it for the neural indicator, measured
in Section~\ref{sec:symgate}.

\subsection{Retraining the point-wise model with the frozen symbolic
  gate}
\label{sec:symgate}

The expression was fitted to the physics label and not to the neural
indicator, so its output is a different routing signal from the one
the mixture was trained against; substituting it at inference alone
would leave a mismatch between the routing statistics seen during
training and at deployment.  The point-wise surrogate is therefore
\emph{retrained} with $p_{s}^{\mathrm{SR}}$ of
Eq.~(\ref{eq:srcalibrated}) frozen as the shock-probability source:
the smooth experts, the shock expert and the gate adapt to the
algebraic routing signal, and the BCE term is removed from the loss,
there being nothing left for it to supervise.

The substitution is not free.  On the point-wise model's own
evaluation protocol (a 10\% random sample of its 156 test
simulations, 4\,068\,074 points, the sample behind the point-wise column of
Table~\ref{tab:results}), the retrained algebraic-gate model reaches
$R^{2} = 0.9421$ for $C_p$ and $0.8202$, $0.7458$ and $0.8400$ for
$C_{fx}$, $C_{fy}$ and $C_{fz}$, against $0.9506$, $0.8512$, $0.8463$
and $0.8555$ for the neural gate.  The pressure field survives the
substitution nearly intact, at a cost of $0.0085$; the lateral
friction does not, losing $0.1005$.  Restricted to the shock subset
the pattern is the same and slightly sharper: $0.8659$ against
$0.8827$ for $C_p$, and $0.7111$ against $0.8292$ for $C_{fy}$.

Two ablations on the same sample indicate how much of what survives
is due to the retraining rather than to the expression.  Evaluating
the retrained checkpoint with the shock probability held at a
constant $0.41$, so that the gate receives no shock information at
all, collapses global $R^{2}(C_{fy})$ to $0.5568$.  Conversely,
driving the \emph{neural} checkpoint with the algebraic routing
signal it was never trained against costs comparatively little
globally ($R^{2}(C_{fy}) = 0.8414$) but drops $C_p$ on shock nodes to
$0.7877$, below the $0.8659$ of the retrained model.  The learned
routing therefore carries information the two physical variables do
not supply, and retraining recovers only part of it.

Two consequences follow for the model of Section~\ref{sec:method}.
First, interpretability of the gate is bought here at a measurable
price in accuracy, chiefly in the lateral friction component: the
substitution is not lossless, and no equivalence between the two
gates is claimed.  Second, the result belongs to the point-wise
feature space.  The spatial indicator consumes the message-passing
latent, which the nine named variables of Eq.~(\ref{eq:srfeatures})
do not span, so this expression cannot be a distillation of it, and
no algebraic gate is used anywhere in the reported model.  Whether an
expression over those same nine variables can instead be fitted to
the spatial indicator's own output is the question of
Section~\ref{sec:srspatial}.

\subsection{Distilling the spatial indicator into the same feature
  space}
\label{sec:srspatial}

The point-wise expression was fitted to the physics label.  The
spatial indicator cannot be treated that way, because what the
reported model routes on is the indicator's own soft output $p_s$ and
not the label; the object to be distilled is therefore $p_s$ itself.
It was evaluated with the reported checkpoint over $15$ training
simulations spanning the flight envelope, $300\,000$ nodes in all, of
which $200\,000$ were given to the search and $100\,000$ held back for
scoring.  The candidate
basis is the same nine variables of Eq.~(\ref{eq:srfeatures}), each
read at the node being predicted, so that any expression found is
strictly local.  That is a deliberate choice, since it is exactly the
locality that the message-passing indicator does not obey.  The
budget is far larger than the point-wise one: $40$ populations of
$100$ expressions over $500$ iterations, a maximum complexity of
$30$, a different operator set,
$\{+,\,-,\,\times,\,\div,\,\exp,\,\tanh,\,\sqrt{\cdot},\,(\cdot)^{2}\}$,
and $196$ minutes of search.  Fitting the indicator's output rather
than the label also removes the calibration step of
Eq.~(\ref{eq:srcalibrated}): the expression already predicts a
probability, and its mean over the held-out simulations, $0.2107$,
matches the neural mean of $0.2069$, so it is used clipped to
$[0,1]$ and otherwise unaltered.

The search independently recovers the core of the point-wise
criterion, and this is worth stating plainly.  Every entry on the
Pareto front from complexity $6$ upward combines an exponential of
$C_{p}^{\mathrm{crit}}$ with the span coordinate $\eta$, which sits
inside that exponential in all but three of them; from
complexity $14$ to the top of the front at $29$ (sixteen
entries), the factor $\exp(C_{p}^{\mathrm{crit}}/\sqrt{\eta})$ is
present in every one of them, literally in ten, as a factor of a
single exponential in an eleventh, and in the remaining five with
$\tanh\sqrt{\eta}$, a scaling constant, or $\eta$ perturbed by a
chord-wise term in place of $\sqrt{\eta}$.  That is
Eq.~(\ref{eq:srdiscovered}) up to the outer squashing and the
exponent on $\eta$, reached against a different target (a network's
soft output rather than the RANS label), for a different model
(spatial rather than point-wise), with a different operator set and a
much larger budget.  The coupling of proximity to the sonic condition
with span-wise station is therefore not an artefact of how the first
search was configured.  What the larger budget adds to that core are
$\mathrm{AoA}_{\sin}$, $n_{z}$ and $x_{\mathrm{norm}}$ terms; the
highest-fidelity expression, at complexity $29$, is
\begin{equation}
  \tilde{p}_{s}^{\mathrm{SR}} = M\,e^{C_{p}^{\mathrm{crit}}}
  \left[
    \exp\!\left(\frac{C_{p}^{\mathrm{crit}}}{\sqrt{\eta}}\right)
    + \mathrm{AoA}_{\sin}\!\left(
        n_{z} + \left|\mathrm{AoA}_{\sin} + n_{z}\right|
        - \exp\tanh\!\left(\frac{\mathrm{AoA}_{\sin}}
                                {0.4628 - x_{\mathrm{norm}}}\right)
      \right)
  \right]^{2}
  \label{eq:srspatial}
\end{equation}
where the tilde distinguishes it from the point-wise gate of
Eq.~(\ref{eq:srcalibrated}) and the absolute value is the search's
own $\sqrt{(\cdot)^{2}}$.  Legibility has clearly been spent: the
chord-wise coordinate absent from the point-wise criterion enters
here, but through a term whose reading is no longer immediate.

The fidelity is nonetheless low.  Eq.~(\ref{eq:srspatial}) reaches
$R^{2} = 0.4095$ against $p_s$ on the $100\,000$ held-out nodes, and
$0.3984$ was already available at complexity $21$, so the last third
of the front buys almost nothing; against the hard label of
Eq.~(\ref{eq:labels}) the same expression scores precision $0.7082$
and recall $0.4510$.  A bare fidelity figure invites the objection
that the search was misconfigured, so it is reported here against a
ceiling.  Two deliberately over-powered models were fitted to the
same targets, over the same nine features and the same
$300\,000$-node sample split $225\,000/75\,000$: gradient boosting
with $400$ trees reaches $R^{2} = 0.8717$ ($F_1 = 0.907$ against the
hard label) and a $256$--$128$--$64$ MLP reaches $R^{2} = 0.8850$
($F_1 = 0.894$).  The information the spatial indicator carries is
thus very largely present in the nine local variables: an
unconstrained function of them recovers $0.8850$ of its variance,
while a $29$-node expression over the identical inputs
recovers $0.4095$, a shortfall of $0.4755$ to the ceiling.  It is the
closed form, not the locality of the features, that cannot hold the
signal.  Budget does not explain the gap either: an earlier, much
shorter search over the same targets stopped at $R^{2} = 0.3671$, and
the long search improves on it by $0.042$ while the ceiling stays
where it is.

What a reader needs from a distillation is not its fidelity but what
substituting it costs, and Table~\ref{tab:symspatial} reports that on
the held-out partition of Table~\ref{tab:results}: $140$ simulations,
$36\,508\,360$ points, with Eq.~(\ref{eq:srspatial}) driving both
places $p_s$ enters the pressure path, namely the gate input and the
scaling of the additive shock residual.  Globally the substitution
costs $0.0116$ of $R^{2}(C_p)$.  On shock nodes it costs $0.1096$,
and the mean absolute error there nearly doubles, from $0.0405$ to
$0.0795$.  The friction columns of the table are a control rather
than an omission: the friction head never reads $p_s$, so its three
scores are unchanged to four decimals under the substitution, which
is what establishes that the pressure deltas are attributable to the
routing signal and to nothing else that moved.  At $0.8253$ on shock
$C_p$ the algebraic gate falls below the collapsed-gate run of
Table~\ref{tab:results} ($0.8814$) and below the point-wise
predecessor on its own sample ($0.8827$): the substitution discards
the whole of the shock-region gain the spatial architecture was built
to obtain.

is six times the size of the point-wise one and was found with a much
larger budget.  Interpretability of the gate grows more expensive as
the indicator grows richer: a shock probability assembled from a
neighbourhood is not, in any form that stays legible, a function of
the node's own named variables, and the ceiling locates the
obstruction in the algebraic hypothesis class rather than in the
features.  The reported model accordingly uses the neural indicator
throughout, and the closed-form criteria of this section are offered
as an analysis of what the gate has learned, not as a component of
it.

\subsection{Aerodynamic coefficient prediction}

Table~\ref{tab:results} reports the coefficient-of-determination
scores on the held-out test set: 140 simulations and 36\,508\,360
surface points, disjoint from both the training partition and the
sixteen simulations on which early stopping selected the checkpoint.
The reported model attains $R^{2}=0.9729$ for the pressure coefficient
and $R^{2}$ between $0.9027$ and $0.9209$ for the three skin-friction
components; restricted to the shock subset the corresponding figures
are $0.9350$ and $0.8692$--$0.9076$.  Table~\ref{tab:mae} states the
same result as mean absolute errors in physical units, and adds the
confidence-weighted $R^{2}$ in which form the reference results for
this database are reported.  In Table~\ref{tab:results},
\emph{collapsed gate} is the run trained before the load-balancing
sign correction, \emph{from scratch} is joint end-to-end training with
the corrected loss, and \emph{two-stage} is the reported model:
end-to-end pre-training followed by a head-only fine-tune with the
backbone frozen.

The gap between pressure and friction accuracy deserves a physical
reading, since it is structural rather than incidental.  The surface
pressure is imposed predominantly by the outer, essentially inviscid
flow; it is therefore a direct and largely local function of the
geometry and flight condition that constitute the input vector.  The
skin friction, in contrast, is a wall-gradient quantity, proportional
to the wall-normal velocity gradient at the base of the boundary
layer: its magnitude is two to three orders smaller than the pressure
loading, and its local value encodes the upstream history of the
boundary layer.  That history comprises transition (fixed at 10\%
chord in the database), pressure-gradient history and incipient
separation, and it is non-local information.  The spatial backbone is
precisely the mechanism that narrows this gap, and the effect is
visible in Table~\ref{tab:results}: against the point-wise formulation
the three friction components gain between $0.056$ and $0.066$ in global
$R^{2}$, compared with $0.022$ for $C_p$.  A residual gap nevertheless
survives, because eight rounds of message passing over a $k{=}8$
neighbour graph propagate information over a bounded number of hops,
whereas boundary-layer history is integrated along a streamline from
the attachment line onward.  That path is neither short in graph
distance nor aligned with the isotropic neighbourhoods the graph
provides.  The lateral component $C_{fy}$ is further penalised because
it is the projection of the shear-stress vector that changes sign with
local surface orientation and cross-flow direction, so its
distribution is centred near zero over large portions of the airframe
and the $R^{2}$ statistic becomes intrinsically more demanding.
Consistently with that reading, $C_{fy}$ is the weakest of the three
friction components in both models, while $C_{fz}$, despite being a
sign-changing projection as well, is marginally the strongest.
Finally, the RANS friction fields are
themselves the least reliable solver output at the extreme
incidences included in the envelope, where convergence
degrades~\cite{Peter2025}, so part of the residual is label noise
rather than model error.  The ranking observed here ($C_p$ best,
then the friction components) matches the reference results for
this database, where every regressor evaluated by
ONERA exhibits the same ordering, with friction $R^{2}$ scores
systematically below the pressure score and worst-case relative
errors on $C_{fy}$ and $C_{fz}$ up to twice those of
$C_p$~\cite{Peter2025}.

The three present-model columns of Table~\ref{tab:results} separate two
independent corrections, and they are not the same size.  The
load-balance term was an entropy that had been added to, rather than
subtracted from, the minimised objective, and it therefore drove the
gate toward the collapse it was meant to prevent.  Holding the
training schedule fixed, repairing its sign moves global $R^{2}(C_p)$
from $0.9549$ to $0.9580$ and the shock-subset score from $0.8814$ to
$0.8956$.  Adding the two-stage schedule on top of the corrected loss
is worth substantially more: $0.9580$ to $0.9729$ globally and
$0.8956$ to $0.9350$ on shock nodes.  Table~\ref{tab:ablation}
collects the two deltas.  Inside the shock region, the regime the
architecture exists to serve, the curriculum is worth about
$2.8\times$ what the sign correction bought.  It is also worth noting
that the collapsed-gate run reaches only $0.8814$ on shock $C_p$, no
better than the $0.8827$ of the point-wise predecessor on its own
sample: the mixture pays for itself only once the gate is balanced.

checkpoint is written at epoch 118 and the run stops on patience at

simulations excluded from selection.

Two further measurements bear on the mechanism rather than on the
schedule.  Suppressing the additive shock residual
$p_s\,f^{\mathrm{shock}}(\mathbf{h}_i)$ at inference, with every
weight otherwise unchanged, raises the $C_p$ mean absolute error on
the shock nodes of eight held-out test simulations (the ablation rows
of Table~\ref{tab:ablation}) from $0.0458$ to $0.1370$: the residual
removes $66.6\%$ of the error the smooth mixture alone would incur
there, and on those same nodes it carries $43.4\%$ of the magnitude of
the smooth output.
This is the explicit discontinuity mechanism being measured directly,
rather than inferred from the aggregate scores.  Against that, the
schedule comparison rests on a single run per arm.  The two arms are
otherwise matched: same data, same loss, same single-process
setup, both stopped on patience rather than at the epoch cap.  Even
so, one run each cannot separate a genuine curriculum effect from a
fortunate initialisation, and repeated seeds are what would settle it.

The subset breakdown of Tables~\ref{tab:results} and \ref{tab:mae}
shows the model performing better in the transonic regime than in the
subsonic one ($R^{2}(C_p) = 0.9842$ against $0.9471$, and a mean
absolute error of $0.0250$ against $0.0415$), which inverts the naive
expectation that shocked flows are the harder problem.  Part of the
$R^{2}$ gap is a variance artefact: the transonic subset carries the
strong suction peaks and the compression jumps, so the denominator of
$R^{2}$ is larger there.  The mean absolute error has no such
denominator and shows the same ordering, however, so the effect is not
purely statistical.  The mechanism is the objective itself.  The shock
label conjoins $C_p^{\mathrm{RANS}} < C_p^{\mathrm{crit}}(M)$ with
$M_{\infty} > 0.75$, and the per-node weight is
$w_i = 1 + \lambda_{\mathrm{sw}}\,y_{\mathrm{shock},i}$ with
$\lambda_{\mathrm{sw}} = 5$, so a shock node is weighted six times a
plain one and \emph{no} subsonic node can ever receive that weight.
Just over a quarter of the held-out transonic points carry the label
(6\,830\,670 of 26\,077\,400), so the optimiser spends a
disproportionate share of its budget in the transonic regime by
construction.  The regime the architecture targets is therefore also
the regime the loss rewards, and the subsonic scores are best read as
the price of that choice rather than as a failure of the model.

subsonic at negative incidence ($M{=}0.30$, $\alpha{=}-6^{\circ}$),
high-subsonic at strong negative incidence
($M{=}0.70$, $\alpha{=}-7.5^{\circ}$), transonic cruise
($M{=}0.80$, $\alpha{=}0^{\circ}$), transonic at moderate incidence
($M{=}0.84$, $\alpha{=}5^{\circ}$), high-transonic
($M{=}0.86$, $\alpha{=}0^{\circ}$), and a high-Mach, high-incidence
condition ($M{=}0.90$, $\alpha{=}6^{\circ}$).  None of the six took
any part in model selection.  In both figures one row is one flight
condition and the three columns are, from left to right, the CFD
truth, the AeroSurrogate prediction and the signed error; all six
rows share a single field scale and a single error scale, so panels
may be compared across conditions.  Figure~\ref{fig:fields_sim147}
then shows the most demanding of the six on its own, with the layout

scale shared between the CFD and model rows, and an error scale
symmetric about zero.

The predicted fields reproduce the characteristic structure of each
regime.  In the subsonic case (top row) the smooth pressure
distribution and the loading redistribution toward the lower surface
induced by the negative incidence are captured without spurious
oscillations; the same inverted loading, stronger, appears in the
second row at $M{=}0.70$, $\alpha{=}-7.5^{\circ}$, where the
lower-surface suction and the leading-edge pressure peaks are
correctly reproduced.  From the third row onward the flow is
transonic: the upper-wing supersonic plateau appears at the correct
chord-wise and span-wise station, and as Mach and incidence increase
down the figure it strengthens, extends outboard and moves aft.  The
fraction of nodes carrying a positive shock label, printed in each row
label, rises from $0.0\%$ in the two subsonic rows through $4.6\%$,
$15.2\%$ and $18.6\%$ to $29.3\%$ in the bottom row; the surrogate
tracks the displacement throughout, including the intensified suction
of the $M{=}0.90$, $\alpha{=}6^{\circ}$ case, which
Figure~\ref{fig:fields_sim147} shows at full resolution.  The
associated skin-friction signature, the marked drop of
$\lvert\mathbf{C}_f\rvert$ over the supersonic upper-surface
region, is recovered in Figure~\ref{fig:fields_cf}.  Because the
friction head predicts a magnitude and a unit direction separately
(Section~\ref{sec:friction}), $\lvert\mathbf{C}_f\rvert$ is a quantity
the model produces directly.

The error maps are signed rather than relative, since an error
normalised by the local coefficient saturates wherever that
coefficient crosses zero and is therefore unreadable over most of the
fuselage.  The scale is
symmetric about zero and shared across all six rows, so white is
exactly zero error and red and blue distinguish under- from
over-prediction.  Two features stand out.  First, the error is not
largest where the shocks are: the two visibly worst rows of
Figures~\ref{fig:fields_cp} and \ref{fig:fields_cf} are the two
lowest-Mach conditions, neither of which carries a single
shock-labelled node, while the four transonic rows are close to white
over most of the wetted surface.  That is the same ordering the
subset scores of Tables~\ref{tab:results} and \ref{tab:mae} report.
Second, within each row the residual error concentrates in a small
number of places: the wing--body junction, the inboard wing, and thin
bands along the leading and trailing edges.  All of them are locations
where the surface field varies over a length scale comparable to the
node spacing, and therefore over few graph hops.  Over the outer wing,
including the supersonic plateau itself, the error is close to zero.
The friction maps of Figure~\ref{fig:fields_cf} follow the same
spatial pattern on a scale two orders of magnitude smaller, consistent
with the mean absolute errors of Table~\ref{tab:mae} and with the
parity plots of Figure~\ref{fig:parity}.

\subsection{Discussion}

Three aspects of the results merit emphasis.  First, the accuracy is
obtained over a wide multi-regime envelope (Mach 0.30--0.96, angle of
attack from $-15^{\circ}$ to $+12.5^{\circ}$ on the held-out
partition) by a single model, without per-condition retraining or
condition-specific calibration, and it is obtained \emph{spatially}.
Replacing the point-wise input vector of the predecessor with an
eight-layer message-passing backbone over the surface graph, at the
cost of growing the model from $3\times 10^{5}$ to 4\,603\,918
parameters, is what produces the gain: $R^{2}(C_p)$ rises from
$0.9506$ to $0.9729$ and $R^{2}(C_{fy})$, the weakest of the four
coefficients, from $0.8463$ to $0.9027$.  The price is a deployment
restriction rather than an accuracy one: the model consumes an
entire surface at once and can no longer score an isolated point.

Second, the two mechanisms that address the smoothing pathology are
now measured rather than asserted (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}).
Removing the additive shock residual at inference, with every weight
otherwise unchanged, leaves the $C_p$ mean absolute error on shock
nodes three times larger; the explicit discontinuity mechanism
therefore accounts for two thirds of the error the smooth mixture
alone would incur there.  The shock-weighted loss is visible in the
subset breakdown as well, and in a direction worth stating plainly:
because only transonic points can carry the shock label, the objective
concentrates its attention where the design targets it, and the
subsonic scores are correspondingly the weaker ones.

Third, the ordering of the two corrections is itself a result.  A sign
error in the load-balance term (an entropy added to, rather than
subtracted from, a minimised objective) collapsed the gate onto a
single expert in the first run; correcting it recovered a healthy
mixture and $+0.0142$ of shock-subset $R^{2}(C_p)$.  Maturing the
backbone first and specialising the heads afterwards, with the
backbone frozen, was worth $+0.0394$ on the same subset, roughly
$2.8\times$ as much.  That comparison rests on a single run per arm,
so it is reported here as a well-supported observation rather than as
an established effect.

\section{CONCLUSIONS}
\label{sec:conclusions}
% ================================================================

A physics-aware surrogate for transonic aerodynamic surface
prediction has been presented, coupling a spatial graph backbone over
the surface mesh to a shock-gated Mixture of Experts head, and
evaluated on 36\,508\,360 held-out RANS surface points of the ONERA
CRM WBPN configuration.  Because the sixteen simulations on which
early stopping selects the checkpoint are themselves drawn from the
test partition, every score quoted here is measured on the remaining
140 simulations, which took no part in model selection.  Six
conclusions are drawn:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Reading the surface as a mesh rather than as a bag of
        independent points is what buys the accuracy.  A backbone of
        eight message-passing layers over a $k$-nearest-neighbour
        graph of the surface nodes, with 4\,603\,918 parameters in
        total, reaches $R^{2}=0.9729$ ($C_p$), $0.9172$ ($C_{fx}$),
        $0.9027$ ($C_{fy}$) and $0.9209$ ($C_{fz}$) globally, and
        $0.9350$, $0.8923$, $0.8692$ and $0.9076$ on the shock
        subset, across Mach $0.30$--$0.96$ and angles of attack from
        $-15^{\circ}$ to $+12.5^{\circ}$ on the held-out partition
        (Table~\ref{tab:results}).
  \item The shock-gated residual expert remains the explicit
        architectural mechanism for the pressure discontinuity, and
        its contribution is measured rather than asserted: removing
        $p_s\,f^{\mathrm{shock}}(\mathbf{h}_i)$ at inference raises
        the $C_p$ mean absolute error on shock nodes from $0.0458$ to
        $0.1370$, so the residual removes $66.6\%$ of the error the
        model would otherwise incur there.  Routing is conditioned on
        the node latent concatenated with its shock probability,
        $[\mathbf{h}_i \,\|\, p_s]$, and not on the freestream Mach
        number.
  \item Two implementation-level corrections separate the reported
        model from an otherwise identical one, and they are not the
        same size.  The load-balancing term was an entropy that had
        been added to, rather than subtracted from, a minimised loss,
        so that it drove the gate onto a single expert.  Correcting
        its sign is worth $+0.0031$ global and
        $+0.0142$ shock $R^{2}(C_p)$ with the training schedule held
        fixed.  Maturing the backbone first and fine-tuning the head
        afterwards with the backbone frozen is worth a further
        $+0.0149$ and $+0.0394$, i.e.\ about $2.8\times$ as much
        inside the shock region, which is the region the architecture
        exists to serve.  This comparison rests on one run per arm,
        so seed variance cannot be excluded.
  \item Training labels derived from real RANS surface pressures via
        the critical-pressure criterion in conjunction with a
        transonic threshold, $C_p < C_p^{\mathrm{crit}}(M)$ and
        $M > 0.75$ (rather than isentropic pseudo-labels computed
        from the inputs), give the shock probability a genuine
        physical meaning while keeping the inference path entirely
        solver-free.  The spatial indicator recovers that label with
        precision $0.8964$ and recall $0.9927$ over eight held-out
        simulations.
  \item Symbolic regression over the point-wise feature space of the
        earlier model, the point-wise baseline of Table~\ref{tab:results},
        compresses its shock criterion into a closed-form expression,
        but the substitution is not free: retraining that model with
        the frozen algebraic gate gives $R^{2}=0.9421$ ($C_p$),
        $0.8202$ ($C_{fx}$), $0.7458$ ($C_{fy}$) and $0.8400$
        ($C_{fz}$), against $0.9506$, $0.8512$, $0.8463$ and $0.8555$
        for the neural gate on the same sample.  Interpretability is
        bought at a real, and for $C_{fy}$ substantial, cost in
        accuracy.  The expression belongs to that feature space; what
        the same distillation yields against the spatial indicator is
        a separate measurement, and the next conclusion reports it.
  \item Distilling the spatial indicator into those same nine local
        variables corroborates the point-wise criterion and, at the
        same time, prices interpretability far higher.  The search
        independently rediscovers the core of
        Eq.~(\ref{eq:srdiscovered}): $C_p^{\mathrm{crit}}$ inside an
        exponential over span-wise station, here as
        $\exp(C_p^{\mathrm{crit}}/\sqrt{\eta})$.  It reaches only
        $R^{2}=0.4095$ against the indicator's output, however, where
        gradient boosting and a small MLP on the identical features
        reach $0.8717$ and $0.8850$: the information is local, and it
        is the closed form
        that cannot carry it.  Substituting the expression for the
        neural indicator costs $0.1096$ of shock-subset $R^{2}(C_p)$
        ($0.9350 \to 0.8253$, Table~\ref{tab:symspatial}) against
        $0.0168$ for the same substitution in the point-wise
        model, $6.5\times$ as much.  The friction scores,
        which do not read $p_s$, are unchanged and serve as the
        control.  No algebraic gate is used in the reported model.
\end{itemize}

Future work will extend the framework to multiple geometries to test
cross-geometry generalisation, incorporate unsteady RANS labels for
buffet-onset prediction, and repeat the two-stage and from-scratch
arms over several initialisations so that the curriculum effect can
be separated from seed variance.  The surrogate remains
differentiable end to end and can still act as a shock-aware penalty
inside adjoint-based aerodynamic shape optimisation loops, but the
spatial formulation raises the cost of doing so: a perturbed geometry
is evaluated as a whole surface with a rebuilt neighbour graph rather
than point by point, so graph construction must either be folded into
the optimisation loop or held fixed under sufficiently small
deformations.  An interpretable sensor for the spatial indicator also
remains open, but Section~\ref{sec:srspatial} narrows what would
count as progress towards one: since an unconstrained model over the
nine local variables recovers most of the indicator's output while a
closed form over the same variables does not, the obstruction lies in
the algebraic hypothesis class and not in the locality of the
features, and a larger symbolic search over that feature set is not
the route.
